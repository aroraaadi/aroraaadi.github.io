What this screen cannot do

The framework it implements asks first for a paradigm shift — a company that is the new thing rather than a division of something older. No filing states that. What a paradigm shift looks like in the numbers is pricing power in a niche the company owns, so the pure-play pillar scores gross margin and whether thematic ETF baskets treat the name as a pure play on something. That is a proxy and it will miss a genuinely new business with thin early margins.

The second test asks for a huge, quantifiable market. Addressable market is not in any statement and every management overstates it, so this scores the computable half: a revenue base small enough that the growth rate still means something. A company adding 25% on $300m has somewhere to go; the same rate on $9bn is a different claim.

The third asks for names institutions have not found. Institutional ownership is a premium data field on this plan, so the screen uses analyst coverage count instead — which is arguably the better proxy, since funds follow sell-side coverage. Zero analysts is scored below two, deliberately: a name nobody covers may be uncoverable.

Financials and real estate are excluded rather than scored. Their balance sheets do not mean what the growth-and-value pillar assumes — leverage is the business — so a net-cash test would rank every bank last for the wrong reason.

And the obvious one: a high score is a statement about reported history and today's price. It is not a forecast, it has no view on management, competition, or what the company said on its last call, and nothing here has been bought.
